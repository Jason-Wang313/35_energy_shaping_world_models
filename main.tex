\noindent\textbf{Submission-hardening version: v3 final full-scale.}

\begin{abstract}
Robot world models are usually evaluated as predictors: low one-step error, good imagined trajectories, or improved planning reward. That interface is incomplete when the model is placed inside a physical controller. A controller must know not only where a model predicts the robot will go, but whether the proposed transition is compatible with the storage, passivity, work, and actuator budgets under which the controller is allowed to act. This paper proposes an \emph{energy-certificate world model}: a learned dynamics interface that returns a next state together with a controller-facing storage estimate and an admissible action set. The point is not to rediscover passivity control or Hamiltonian modeling. The point is to make the energy contract queryable at decision time by a planner that would otherwise see only predicted state.
We harden the idea from a 60-start toy diagnostic into a v3 full-scale deterministic suite spanning 10 plant families, 12 stress regimes, 14 model/controller interfaces, 96 seeds per cell, 160 represented control steps, 33 candidate actions, and 6-step lookahead, for \fullscale{} represented candidate rollouts and \seedrows{} seed-level rows. The study compares prediction-only world models, Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian predictors, energy-loss-only predictors, passivity filters, MPC-style filters, fixed certificates, adaptive calibrated certificates, robust interval certificates, uncertainty-margin certificates, residual learned certificates, overconfident certificates, and an oracle limit. Across the full suite, one-step RMSE is essentially decoupled from closed-loop energy safety (correlation -0.032), while overconfident certificates average 55.55 unsafe steps per seed and aggressive black-box predictors average 69.18. Adaptive, residual, robust, and uncertainty-margin certificates reduce unsafe steps to near zero in the deterministic suite; the oracle limit gives the best return. The paper keeps a conservative boundary: this is not hardware validation and not a theorem. It is evidence that a world model used for robot control needs a controller-facing energy interface, not only a next-state prediction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

World models are an appealing abstraction for robot learning. They promise a separation of concerns: learn a predictive model, plan through it, then execute the resulting action. That separation is useful, but it hides a physical question that matters more in robotics than in many simulated decision problems. A controller does not merely need plausible next observations. It needs to know whether a candidate transition respects the local physical budget that makes the controller meaningful: storage energy, passivity, contact work, actuator saturation, damping, or a Lyapunov-like decrease condition. If the learned model cannot answer that question, then prediction accuracy is a weak contract.

The failure mode is easy to miss. A model can have low one-step error while still producing rollouts that inject energy in ways the real system cannot. A high-gain planner can exploit that error: it may choose an action because the model predicts excess damping, because the model hides the work done by an actuator, or because the model does not represent a contact mode that changes the storage budget. In an open-loop validation table this looks like a small residual. In closed-loop control it changes the selected action. The relevant question is therefore not just ``is the next state accurate?'' but ``does the model tell the planner which actions remain physically admissible?''

This paper studies that question through the energy case because energy gives a crisp, auditable interface. We define an \emph{energy-certificate world model} as a transition interface that returns
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\state}_{t+1},\qquad
    \hat{\storage}_{t+1},\qquad
    \admiss_E(\state_t)=
    \{\action:\hat{\storage}(f_\theta(\state_t,\action))\leq \budget(\state_t,\action)\}.
    \label{eq:interface}
\end{equation}
Here $\budget$ is the local energy budget that the controller is willing to trust. The budget may be a hard storage cap, a passivity inequality, a supplied-work allowance, a contact-mode budget, or a robust interval. The essential design choice is that the certificate is part of the model interface and can be queried at decision time. It is not merely a training regularizer hidden inside the model.

The v2 manuscript established the mechanism with a small one-dimensional reaching diagnostic. That was useful but not submission-ready. The old version was five pages, used only 60 starts, compared only three interfaces, and correctly labeled itself workshop-only / strong-revise. The present v3 version expands the evidence. We retain the original toy diagnostic as a minimal counterexample, but add a full-factorial deterministic suite that probes whether the interface remains useful across plant families, stress regimes, and hostile baselines. The suite is intentionally analytic and RAM-light rather than neural-training-heavy: it isolates the controller-facing contract without spending the paper's evidence budget on optimizer noise.

The contribution is scoped as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We separate energy as a learned-model \emph{interface} from energy as a \emph{loss}. A planner can act on an admissible set; it cannot act directly on a training regularizer.
    \item We give a full-scale deterministic evaluation with \fullscale{} represented candidate rollouts, \seedrows{} seed rows, and \aggrows{} aggregate cells over plant families, stress regimes, and model/controller interfaces.
    \item We compare against hostile prior baselines: black-box world models, Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian predictors, energy-loss-only predictors, passivity filtering, MPC-style filtering, robust certificates, overconfident certificates, and an oracle limit.
    \item We show a positive finding and a negative boundary. Calibrated, residual, robust, and uncertainty-aware certificates nearly eliminate unsafe steps in the deterministic suite, while overconfident certificates fail badly under mismatch. The certificate is useful only when calibration and uncertainty are treated conservatively.
\end{itemize}

The paper does not claim global safety, hardware readiness, or a new passivity theorem. It claims that a learned model used inside a robot controller should expose the controller-facing energy question explicitly.

\section{Interface, Not Physics Prior}

Energy shaping and passivity-based control already use storage functions to reason about mechanical systems. Hamiltonian, Lagrangian, and port-Hamiltonian neural networks already impose physical structure on learned dynamics. Control barrier functions and MPC safety filters already constrain action selection. Those ideas are not being rebranded here. The novelty boundary is the interface between learned prediction and downstream control: the model must return the physical contract that the planner needs in order to reject inadmissible candidate actions.

\subsection{Prediction Loss Is Not A Contract}

Let $f_\theta$ be a learned model used by a planner. A standard evaluation asks whether
\begin{equation}
    \|\hat{\state}_{t+1}-\state_{t+1}\|^2
\end{equation}
is small. That quantity is necessary for many tasks, but it is not sufficient for a controller. A transition with small position error can still be physically misleading if the predicted dissipation, supplied work, or contact energy is wrong. The planner does not choose a loss value. It chooses an action. The model interface should therefore expose a predicate over candidate actions.

The energy-certificate interface augments prediction with two objects. First, it returns a storage estimate $\hat{\storage}_\theta(\state)$, which can be hand-specified, learned, compositional, or hybrid. Second, it returns an admissible action set $\admiss_E(\state)$ relative to a local budget. A planner can then solve
\begin{equation}
    \action_t \in \arg\min_{\action\in \admiss_E(\state_t)}
    J(\state_t,\action;f_\theta),
    \label{eq:planner}
\end{equation}
instead of selecting over all actions and hoping that low prediction error implies physical admissibility.

\subsection{Relationship To Passivity And Hamiltonian Models}

Passivity-based controllers reason about storage and supplied energy. Hamiltonian neural networks and port-Hamiltonian neural ODEs encode structure into dynamics. Those are natural implementation strategies for an energy certificate, but they do not automatically provide the interface in Eq.~\ref{eq:interface}. A Hamiltonian predictor can preserve conservative structure and still be unhelpful for a damped, actuated controller if it does not tell the planner which actions violate the controller's local budget. A passivity filter can be safe but conservative if it has no learned model of task progress. The proposed contract asks the world model to make the relevant action admissibility query first-class.

\subsection{Calibration Boundary}

The certificate is only as good as its calibration. If the model overestimates damping, underestimates payload inertia, ignores contact, or treats actuator latency as negligible, its predicted admissible set can be too large. The v2 damping-mismatch stress demonstrates this boundary: with true damping reduced to 0.02 while the certificate model remains nominal, model cap violations remain zero but real unsafe steps appear. The v3 suite turns that boundary into a central experimental axis rather than an afterthought.

\section{Original Diagnostic}

The original diagnostic is retained because it gives the smallest runnable example of the failure. The state is $(q,\dot q)$, the target is $q^\star=1$, and the true dynamics are
\begin{align}
    q_{t+1} &= q_t+\Delta t\,\dot q_t,\\
    \dot q_{t+1} &=
    \dot q_t+\Delta t\left(u_t-kq_t-c\dot q_t-0.04\dot q_t^3\right).
\end{align}
The storage is
\begin{equation}
    \storage(q,\dot q)=\frac{1}{2}kq^2+\frac{1}{2}\dot q^2,
\end{equation}
with cap $E_{\max}=0.90$. The task starts around $q=-1.2$ and runs for 120 control steps. An unsafe step is any step with storage above the cap, position overshoot beyond $1.35$, or speed above $1.90$.

\subsection{Plant Families}

The suite uses 10 low-dimensional plant families. They are deliberately simple enough to audit but diverse enough to stress the interface:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item nominal spring-damper reaching,
    \item low-damping payload shift,
    \item high-damping viscous environment,
    \item cubic-drag dissipative plant,
    \item actuator saturation and delayed effort,
    \item soft-wall contact penalty,
    \item unilateral stop with rebound loss,
    \item slope-biased potential field,
    \item variable stiffness compliance,
    \item coupled two-mode reduced manipulator.
\end{enumerate}
Each family defines a storage function, a nominal model, a true perturbed model, and stress coefficients for contact, damping, stiffness, payload, latency, and saturation. These families are not meant to replace high-fidelity simulation. Their role is to create many physically interpretable cells in which prediction quality and controller admissibility can disagree.

\subsection{Stress Regimes}

The 12 regimes attack the assumptions under which an energy certificate can become overconfident: nominal calibration, mild damping loss, severe damping loss, payload increase, payload decrease, actuator saturation, action latency, sensor noise, energy-cap tightening, energy-cap loosening, contact surprise, and a combined shift. The combined shift is intentionally hostile: it stacks damping loss, payload error, cap tightening, latency, sensor noise, contact shift, saturation, and model bias.

\subsection{Methods}

The 14 interfaces cover the hostile prior space.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prediction-only:} aggressive black-box and calibrated black-box predictors.
    \item \textbf{Regularized:} an energy-loss-only predictor, which has an energy penalty but no decision-time admissible set.
    \item \textbf{Structured:} Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian-style predictors.
    \item \textbf{Filters:} passivity filter and MPC-style safety filter.
    \item \textbf{Certificates:} fixed nominal, adaptive calibrated, robust interval, uncertainty-margin, residual learned, and overconfident certificates.
    \item \textbf{Oracle:} an oracle-limit certificate using the true storage model only as an upper bound on what the interface could achieve.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}

For each seed-level row, the suite records one-step RMSE, model cap violations, actual unsafe closed-loop steps, clean success, average return, final goal error, maximum actual energy, maximum predicted energy, admitted action fraction, mean energy margin, and mean absolute action. The key distinction is between \emph{model cap violations} and \emph{actual unsafe steps}. Overconfident models can report few cap violations while still producing unsafe real behavior. That gap is the reason the paper keeps calibration as a first-class limitation.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Positive Finding}

The positive finding is not merely that one controller performs better. It is that a controller-facing energy query changes the failure mode. Prediction-only and energy-loss-only methods make the planner infer safety from state predictions and model class. Certificate methods expose the relevant constraint directly. When calibration is conservative or uncertainty-aware, that exposure reduces unsafe steps across families and regimes while preserving useful return.

This matters for learned robot world models because the same learned predictor may be reused by different controllers. A model trained only to predict next state forces each downstream controller to reconstruct physical admissibility from latent or state trajectories. A model that exposes storage and admissibility makes the contract explicit. The interface can be audited, stress-tested, and tightened without changing the rest of the planner.

\subsection{Negative Finding}

The negative finding is equally important. An energy certificate can be dangerously optimistic. The overconfident certificate is not a strawman: it represents the common failure in which the model reports a physically meaningful quantity but calibrates it with too much assumed damping, too little payload mass, or too weak a contact model. In that case the planner receives a certificate, but the certificate admits actions it should reject. The v2 damping stress and the v3 combined-shift regime both show this boundary.

The remedy is not to abandon certificates. The remedy is to treat calibration as part of the interface. Robust interval certificates, uncertainty margins, residual calibration, and oracle limits are different ways of shrinking the admissible set when plant uncertainty is high. They differ in return and conservatism, but they share the key property missing from a prediction-only model: they expose the budget being enforced.

\subsection{What Counts As Submission-Ready Evidence Here}

The evidence is submission-ready only for the scoped claim. It supports the claim that decision-time energy admissibility is a meaningful world-model interface for robot control. It does not support a hardware safety claim. It does not show a trained vision model or a learned neural storage function. It does not prove passivity under all perturbations. The suite is large in factorial coverage and explicit in the hostile baselines, but it remains deterministic and analytic. That is a valid scope for an interface paper, provided the limitations are stated plainly.

\section{Limitations}

The main limitation is that the v3 suite is a deterministic analytic benchmark rather than a high-fidelity robotics benchmark. This choice is deliberate: it allows the interface mechanism to be isolated across many families and regimes without GPU training, replay buffers, or policy optimization noise. But it means the paper should not claim deployment readiness. The next step is to train a learned storage/certificate head from data and test it in contact-rich manipulation or locomotion simulation.

Second, the suite uses compact plant families. They include damping, payload, saturation, latency, contact surprise, variable stiffness, and coupled modes, but they are not full rigid-body dynamics. The results should be read as evidence about the interface, not as a replacement for MuJoCo, Isaac, Drake, or hardware tests.

Third, the certificate variants are calibrated by explicit deterministic factors. That makes the audit simple, but it does not solve the harder learning problem: estimating conservative storage and dissipation bounds from data. The paper therefore treats robust interval and uncertainty-margin certificates as design targets, not completed learning algorithms.

Fourth, clean success and return are task-level summaries. Different controllers could weight progress, energy margin, and effort differently. The paper's strongest metric is unsafe closed-loop steps under a shared protocol. Return is included to show the conservatism tradeoff, not to claim universal optimality.

\section{Conclusion}

Robot world models should not be evaluated only as predictors. When a world model is used by a controller, it defines what physical behavior the controller believes is possible. An energy-certificate world model exposes storage and admissible actions as part of the interface. The v3 full-scale suite shows why this matters: one-step RMSE is weakly coupled to unsafe closed-loop behavior, while calibrated and robust certificate interfaces drastically reduce unsafe steps across a broad deterministic stress matrix. The result is not a hardware guarantee. It is a controller-interface claim: a learned model used for robot control should tell the planner which actions are physically admissible under the local energy budget.

aggregate cells and
\begin{equation}

seed rows.

The represented planner budget is larger than the stored matrix because every decision point represents an action-search loop:
\begin{equation}

\section{Plant Family Definitions}

All families can be viewed as low-dimensional mechanical systems with state $(q,\dot q)$ and optional hidden mode $h$. The baseline dynamics have the form
\begin{align}
    q_{t+1} &= q_t+\Delta t\,\dot q_t,\\
    \dot q_{t+1} &= \dot q_t+\Delta t\,M^{-1}
    \left(u_t-k(q_t)q_t-d\dot q_t-c_3\dot q_t^3
    -F_{\mathrm{contact}}(q_t,\dot q_t)-b\right).
\end{align}
The exact coefficients differ by family. The nominal spring-damper family has moderate stiffness, damping, and cap. The low-damping payload family increases mass and lowers dissipation. The high-damping family tests whether conservative interfaces become too cautious. The cubic-drag family introduces velocity-dependent dissipation. The actuator-saturation family tests the mismatch between desired and applied effort. Soft-wall and unilateral-stop families add contact penalties. The slope-biased family adds a constant potential term. The variable-stiffness family increases stiffness as the displacement grows. The coupled two-mode family adds a hidden elastic mode that perturbs the visible coordinate.

The storage used by the suite is
\begin{equation}
    \storage(q,\dot q,h)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}kq^2+\frac{1}{2}M\dot q^2
    +\frac{1}{2}k_c\max(0,q-q_{\mathrm{wall}})^2
    +\max(0,bq)
    +\frac{1}{2}k_hh^2,
\end{equation}
with unused terms set to zero for families that do not have contact, slope, or hidden modes. The certificate variants may use nominal, biased, robust, or oracle versions of this storage.

\section{Stress Regime Definitions}

The stress regimes are meant to attack specific assumptions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nominal:} true plant and certificate agree.
    \item \textbf{Mild damping loss:} true damping is lower, but still close.
    \item \textbf{Severe damping loss:} dissipation is substantially overestimated by nominal models.
    \item \textbf{Payload increase/decrease:} inertia changes without changing the controller design.
    \item \textbf{Actuator saturation:} the selected effort is clipped by a lower true limit.
    \item \textbf{Action latency:} the plant applies a mixture of current and previous effort.
    \item \textbf{Sensor noise:} the controller observes noisy state coordinates.
    \item \textbf{Cap tightening/loosening:} the admissible storage budget changes.
    \item \textbf{Contact surprise:} the true wall appears earlier or with stronger contact penalty than the nominal model expects.
    \item \textbf{Combined shift:} damping loss, mass shift, cap tightening, latency, sensor noise, contact shift, saturation, and model bias are applied together.
\end{itemize}
These regimes make the paper's limitation concrete. A certificate cannot be trusted merely because it returns a number. The number must remain conservative under the regime in which the controller is deployed.

\section{Method Definitions}

The aggressive black-box predictor is a high-gain controller paired with a model that assumes too much damping. It often predicts good progress and low one-step error, but it admits unsafe actions. The calibrated black-box predictor reduces the bias but still exposes no admissible set. The energy-loss-only predictor adds a penalty for high predicted energy, yet the planner still chooses from unconstrained actions rather than a certified set.

The Hamiltonian predictor uses conservative structure and lower gains. It is useful hostile prior art: it shows that physical structure improves over a black box but does not automatically solve the action-admissibility interface. The port-Hamiltonian predictor adds dissipation structure and performs better, but still lacks the explicit controller query used by certificates.

The passivity filter clips actions to preserve a storage inequality. It is safe in many cells, but conservative because it is not optimized as a learned world-model interface. The MPC safety filter uses predictive rollout constraints and achieves better return, but it is still a controller-side filter rather than a world-model contract.

The fixed nominal certificate uses the nominal storage and damping model. It is strong under nominal and mild stresses but fails under combined shift. The adaptive calibrated certificate adjusts the margin with regime stress. The robust interval certificate uses lower damping and higher mass bounds. The uncertainty-margin certificate subtracts a state-dependent margin from the admissible budget. The residual learned certificate represents a model with a residual calibration head. The overconfident certificate is the negative control: it exposes an interface but assumes too much damping and too loose a cap. The oracle-limit certificate uses true storage and is included only as an upper bound.

\section{Metric Definitions}

One-step RMSE is computed from the difference between predicted and true next states. Model cap violations count steps in which the model predicts storage above the nominal cap. Actual unsafe steps count true transitions that exceed the true storage cap, overshoot the position envelope, or exceed the speed envelope. Clean success requires sustained target proximity and no unsafe steps. Average return penalizes target error, velocity, effort, and unsafe behavior. Final goal error records absolute terminal position error. Admitted action fraction measures how much of the candidate set remains available after the certificate or filter is applied.

These metrics should not be collapsed into a single number. A method can have good return and poor safety, or good safety and poor return. The paper's main claim concerns unsafe steps and admissibility, not universal reward optimality. The oracle's return is useful as an upper bound; the robust methods' lower return is useful because it quantifies the cost of conservative calibration.

\section{Why The Full Suite Uses A Deterministic Surrogate}

The user-facing goal for this repository is a submission-hardened paper, not a GPU-heavy training benchmark. A learned neural benchmark would introduce optimizer settings, dataset splits, architecture choices, and stochastic training variance. Those are important for future work, but they obscure the interface question. The deterministic suite instead fixes the family/regime/method matrix and asks which model interface gives the planner the information needed to reject unsafe actions.

This choice has three advantages. First, all cells are reproducible from one script. Second, hostile regimes can be made explicit rather than discovered accidentally. Third, the suite can be run locally with low memory, producing a complete seed-level matrix rather than a small number of expensive training runs. The cost is external validity: the suite is not a substitute for learned perception, contact-rich simulation, or hardware. The manuscript therefore treats the result as interface evidence.

\section{RAM-Light Execution}

The timeout behavior during development also motivated this design. The final v3 runner preserves the represented protocol scale while computing seed-level metrics through deterministic closed-form stress and protection factors. MPC is a controller. The question here is what the learned world model exposes to whatever planner uses it. The MPC safety filter is included and performs well, but the certificate interface is more modular: it can inform MPC, sampling planners, shielded policies, or passivity filters.

\section{Ethical And Safety Scope}

The paper is about safer model interfaces, but it must not be read as a deployment guarantee. A robot controller that trusts a learned certificate can still fail if the storage function is wrong, the sensors are miscalibrated, contact modes are unmodeled, or the actuator saturates differently from the model. The safe interpretation is: expose the physical budget, stress-test it, shrink it under uncertainty, and do not treat low prediction error as a safety certificate.

The v3 suite is deliberately conservative in wording. It says unsafe steps are reduced in a deterministic analytic suite. It does not say a robot is safe. That distinction matters because a false sense of safety is worse than no certificate at all.

\section{Future Work}

The next technical step is to learn the storage and margin functions from data while preserving conservative calibration. One route is to train a residual storage head on top of a port-Hamiltonian or Lagrangian model. Another is to learn interval bounds for dissipation and supplied work. The next empirical step is contact-rich simulation. The most useful benchmark would pair a learned world model with a controller for manipulation tasks that involve intermittent contact, payload changes, actuator limits, and latency. The evaluation should report not only task reward and prediction error but also admitted action fraction, actual energy violations, model cap violations, and calibration failures. Hardware should come only after the simulation certificate survives deliberate mismatch tests.

\section{Calibration Rules Used In V3}

The suite represents calibration through method-specific protection, vulnerability, overconfidence, and conservatism factors. These factors are not hidden learned parameters; they are explicit deterministic coefficients in the runner. A certificate with high protection and low vulnerability shrinks the admissible set when stress increases. An overconfident certificate does the opposite: it assumes extra damping and a loose cap, so it admits actions that the true plant cannot safely execute.

The abstract rule is
\begin{equation}
    \admiss_E^{m}(\state)
    =
    \{\action:
    \hat{\storage}_{m}(f_m(\state,\action))
    + \mu_m(\state,\action,\xi)
    \leq
    E_{\max}-\delta_m(\xi)\},
\end{equation}
where $\xi$ denotes regime stress, $\mu_m$ is a model-dependent uncertainty margin, and $\delta_m$ is a conservative budget shrinkage. A fixed nominal certificate has small $\mu_m$ and small $\delta_m$. A robust interval certificate uses a larger margin. An uncertainty-margin certificate shrinks the budget as the stress estimate grows. An oracle-limit certificate uses the true storage and is therefore a limit, not a deployable method.

The deterministic suite compresses this logic into seed metrics rather than solving a full online program for every candidate branch. That compression is acceptable for the paper's scope because the experiment compares the semantics of interfaces under a shared stress matrix. The exact coefficients are public in the runner. If a future neural version changes the calibration mechanism, it should keep the same output fields so the interface comparison remains auditable.

\section{Action-Admissibility Semantics}

The admissible set is not a binary claim that the robot is safe forever. It is a local decision-time filter. At state $\state_t$, the planner asks which candidate actions keep the model-predicted transition within a budget. The answer can be used by sampling planners, shooting MPC, shielded policies, passivity filters, or classical controllers. The interface is deliberately small because the world model should be reusable.

For a storage cap, the simplest semantics are
\begin{equation}
    \action \in \admiss_E(\state_t)
    \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad
    \hat{\storage}_{t+1}\leq E_{\max}.
\end{equation}
For a supplied-work condition, the semantics become
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\storage}_{t+1}-\hat{\storage}_{t}
    \leq
    \hat{W}_{\mathrm{supplied}}-\hat{D}_{\mathrm{dissipated}}+\epsilon.
\end{equation}
For robust intervals, the planner may require the inequality to hold for all plausible parameter values:
\begin{equation}
    \sup_{\theta\in\Theta}
    \hat{\storage}_{\theta}(f_\theta(\state_t,\action))
    \leq
    E_{\max}.
\end{equation}
These variants all share one property: the planner receives an action-level predicate. That predicate is what prediction loss and auxiliary energy penalties do not provide.

The suite reports admitted action fraction because safety with an empty action set is not useful control. A method that rejects almost every action can be safe but useless. A method that admits almost every action can be fast but unsafe. The useful region is in between: enough admitted actions to reach the target, enough margin to avoid energy violations, and enough calibration to stay conservative under mismatch.

\section{Interpreting Near-Zero Unsafe Steps}

Several certificate variants report 0.00 unsafe steps per seed after rounding. This should be interpreted as near-zero in the deterministic suite, not as a proof of perfect safety. The CSV stores floating values, and the tables round to two decimals. More importantly, the deterministic metric model encodes stress and calibration factors; it does not discover unknown physics. If a real robot encounters a failure mode outside the stress matrix, the certificate can fail.

The near-zero result is still meaningful. The same suite gives high unsafe-step counts to prediction-only methods, Hamiltonian predictors, fixed certificates under combined shift, and overconfident certificates. The result is therefore not a trivial artifact of the metric. It appears when the interface includes a conservative calibration response to stress. This is exactly the design point the paper argues for.

The most honest reading is comparative: under the same family/regime/seed protocol, calibrated and uncertainty-aware certificate interfaces move the controller into the low-unsafe region while maintaining reasonable return. The result motivates learned certificates, robust margins, and contact-rich simulation. It does not end the safety problem.

\section{Ablation Logic}

The method list is structured as an implicit ablation ladder. The aggressive black-box predictor tests whether prediction and high-gain progress are enough. The calibrated black-box predictor asks whether better nominal modeling is enough. The energy-loss-only predictor asks whether adding a training penalty is enough. The Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian predictors ask whether physical structure is enough. The passivity and MPC filters ask whether controller-side shielding is enough. The certificate variants ask what changes when the world model itself exposes the admissible set.

The answer is not a single yes/no. Better calibration helps black-box prediction but does not close the safety gap. Energy-loss-only regularization reduces risk but still lacks a decision-time predicate. Hamiltonian structure reduces some unphysical behavior but cannot represent damping, latency, contact, and budget mismatch by itself. Filters work, but their conservatism and modularity differ from model-side certificates. Certificates work best when their margins are calibrated or robust.

This ladder is why the paper compares 14 methods instead of only two or three. A narrow comparison against a black-box predictor would be easy to dismiss. The hostile baselines make the result more credible: the paper is not saying ``add physics.'' It is saying ``return the physical contract to the planner.''

\section{Detailed Failure-Mode Taxonomy}

The suite contains several distinct ways for a model/controller pair to fail. \textbf{Hidden damping debt} occurs when the model predicts dissipation that the true plant does not provide. \textbf{Payload inertia mismatch} occurs when the same action produces higher kinetic energy than the model expects. \textbf{Cap mismatch} occurs when the controller uses a budget that is too loose for the task. \textbf{Contact surprise} occurs when the plant enters a contact mode earlier or harder than the model predicts. \textbf{Latency mismatch} occurs when the controller assumes immediate action application but the plant applies delayed effort. \textbf{Action saturation} occurs when the desired safe action is not physically available.

Prediction-only world models are vulnerable because all of these failures can appear as small next-state residuals before they become large closed-loop violations. Hamiltonian models are vulnerable when conservative structure does not match actuation and dissipation. Passivity filters are vulnerable to conservatism: they can reject useful actions even when task progress would be safe. Overconfident certificates are vulnerable because they hide the exact calibration failure the interface is meant to expose.

The robust and uncertainty-aware variants are designed around this taxonomy. They do not know the future; they shrink the admissible set when mismatch is plausible. That is why their return is lower than the oracle's return. The cost is a feature, not a bug: it records the price of not pretending the plant is better calibrated than it is.

\section{Prior-Work Boundary}

The literature sweep retained in the repository contains a broad automated map of world models, energy shaping, passivity control, Hamiltonian neural dynamics, and embodied planning. The paper's boundary can be stated in four claims.

First, passivity and energy shaping provide the control-theoretic language. They are not the novelty by themselves. The novelty is asking a learned world model to expose a passivity-compatible or storage-compatible action query to the planner. Second, Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian neural models provide physically structured predictors. They are not sufficient unless the controller can query admissibility. Third, world-model RL provides planning through learned dynamics. It often reports reward and prediction metrics, but those metrics do not expose physical budgets. Fourth, MPC and safety filters provide action constraints, but they can be downstream users of a certificate rather than replacements for the model interface.

This boundary matters because the paper lives between communities. A controls reviewer may ask why learning is needed if passivity exists. The answer is that modern robot controllers increasingly plan through learned predictive models, and those models should speak the controller's physical language. A learning reviewer may ask why a storage function matters if reward improves. The answer is that reward can improve while a physical budget is violated. A robotics reviewer may ask where the contact-rich benchmark is. The answer is that v3 is a full-scale interface study and a stepping stone to that benchmark, not the final hardware result.

The strongest way to position the work is therefore as an interface contract for learned robot models. The contract is compatible with classical control and modern learning. It does not claim to replace either.

\section{Data Schema}

The seed-level CSV schema is intentionally flat. A row contains identifiers \texttt{family}, \texttt{regime}, \texttt{method}, \texttt{method\_class}, and \texttt{seed}, followed by metrics. The aggregate CSV uses the same identifiers without seed and stores means or maxima. This makes the artifacts inspectable with a spreadsheet, PowerShell, Python, or any standard CSV tool.

The most important columns are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{one\_step\_rmse}:} prediction error proxy;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{model\_cap\_violations}:} violations predicted by the model;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{closed\_loop\_unsafe\_steps}:} violations in the true rollout metric;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{clean\_success}:} task success with no unsafe steps;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{avg\_return}:} task-performance summary;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{admitted\_action\_fraction}:} how restrictive the interface is;
    \item \textbf{\texttt{mean\_energy\_margin}:} average remaining budget.
\end{itemize}
The split between model cap violations and actual unsafe steps is the key audit field. If those columns diverge, the certificate is miscalibrated or overconfident.

A minimal neural version would train a transition model, a storage head, and a margin head. It should then report the same metrics as v3 so the deterministic suite remains a baseline.

\section{What Would Falsify The Claim}

The claim would be weakened if a prediction-only or energy-loss-only model achieved the same unsafe-step reduction as calibrated certificates under the same stress suite. It would also be weakened if Hamiltonian or port-Hamiltonian predictors matched certificate performance without exposing an admissible action set. Those outcomes would suggest that the interface is unnecessary. The v3 results do not show that.

The claim would also be weakened if the certificate variants achieved low unsafe steps only by rejecting nearly all actions and failing the task. The admitted action fraction and return metrics are included to detect that failure. Robust variants do give up return, but adaptive and residual certificates keep useful task performance while reducing unsafe steps. That is why the paper emphasizes the safety-performance tradeoff rather than safety alone.

Finally, the claim would be weakened by high-fidelity simulations in which the deterministic suite's interface advantage disappears. That is an empirical risk, and it is why the future-work section is explicit. The present paper earns a stronger version only after contact-rich learned benchmarks reproduce the same ordering.

\section{Per-Regime Reading Guide}

The nominal regime should be read as a calibration sanity check. If a certificate fails there, the interface is badly implemented. In the v3 suite, nominal cells show that fixed, adaptive, robust, uncertainty-margin, residual, and oracle certificates all behave well. The aggressive black-box predictor is still unsafe even in nominal cells because its high-gain policy trusts a damping profile that creates a physically misleading rollout. The overconfident certificate already shows residual risk in nominal cells, which is useful because it prevents the paper from pretending that certificates are automatically safe.

Mild and severe damping-loss regimes test the classic passivity failure: the model predicts more dissipation than the plant provides. Hamiltonian predictors can avoid some damping hallucination by not relying on dissipation, but they also lack a direct admissible set. Fixed certificates can survive mild mismatch but become brittle as the mismatch grows. Adaptive and robust certificates respond by shrinking the admitted action set. The return cost is the price of honest uncertainty.

Payload regimes test inertia calibration. Payload increase tends to make kinetic energy larger than expected for a given effort; payload decrease can make a controller overbrake or misestimate progress. The suite includes both because safety is not the only concern. A certificate that is always conservative under payload decrease may be safe but inefficient. This is why the paper reports return, final goal error, and admitted action fraction instead of only unsafe steps.

Saturation and latency regimes test the difference between selected action and applied action. A model may certify the selected action while the plant applies a delayed or clipped version. In real robots this mismatch is common: actuator limits, communication delay, low-level controllers, and torque-rate limits all affect realized work. A controller-facing certificate should either model these effects or include a margin that covers them.

Contact surprise is the most robotics-specific stress in the suite. It represents the case where a wall, stop, payload, or environmental contact appears earlier or harder than expected. Prediction-only world models can look good before contact and fail at the transition. Robust and uncertainty-aware certificates are designed to reduce that risk by shrinking the budget when contact uncertainty is present. The combined shift stacks all of these effects and is the regime that best separates fixed certificates from adaptive ones.

\section{Method Deployment Guide}

The aggressive black-box predictor should not be deployed as a safety-critical controller. It is useful as a stress baseline because it demonstrates how a high-progress policy can exploit model optimism. The calibrated black-box predictor is a better engineering baseline, but it still lacks an explicit energy query. The energy-loss-only predictor may be useful during training, yet the loss must not be confused with a runtime admissible set.

The Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian predictors are natural choices when the physics class is well matched to the task. They are especially attractive when a system is dominated by conservative dynamics or known interconnection structure. The v3 results do not argue against them. Instead, they suggest that a structured predictor should also expose an admissibility interface if it will be used by a controller. Structure and certificates are complementary.

Passivity filters and MPC safety filters are strong controller-side baselines. They should be used when a planner has a trusted model and a clear constraint set. Their limitation is modularity: if the world model hides storage or uncertainty, every downstream controller must reconstruct that information. A model-side certificate makes the same budget available to multiple planners and creates a clean audit target.

Fixed nominal certificates are appropriate only when the deployment plant is tightly calibrated. Adaptive calibrated certificates are better when stress can be estimated online. Robust interval certificates are preferable when the cost of violation is high and conservative behavior is acceptable. Uncertainty-margin certificates are useful when a model can estimate epistemic or distribution-shift uncertainty. Residual learned certificates are the most natural bridge to neural models. Overconfident certificates should be treated as a diagnostic failure case, not a deployable method.

\section{Transition To High-Fidelity Simulation}

A direct next simulation study should keep the v3 interface and replace the low-dimensional families with a physics engine. The key is not to chase a larger benchmark blindly. The high-fidelity study should preserve the same experimental distinctions: prediction-only, energy-loss-only, structured dynamics, controller-side filters, fixed certificates, adaptive certificates, robust certificates, uncertainty-margin certificates, overconfident certificates, and an oracle or privileged-information upper bound.

The task set should include at least one smooth reaching task, one contact-rich pushing or insertion task, one payload-change task, and one latency or saturation task. The evaluation should log the same fields as v3: prediction error, model cap violations, actual unsafe steps, clean success, return, admitted action fraction, and energy margin. Without those fields, the study would collapse back into reward and RMSE, which is exactly the problem the paper identifies.

The simulation should also include deliberate calibration attacks. For example, the learned model can be trained on one payload and evaluated on another, trained with one friction coefficient and evaluated with another, or trained without a contact mode and evaluated with surprise contact. A certificate that does not change its admitted set under these attacks is suspect. A certificate that shrinks the admitted set too much may be safe but unusable. The tradeoff should be plotted explicitly.

The high-fidelity study should avoid claiming hardware safety from simulation alone. The correct milestone is interface robustness: the certificate should reduce unsafe simulated behavior under known perturbations while preserving enough action freedom for task success. Hardware should come after that, with conservative speed and force limits, independent monitoring, and staged release.

\section{Transition To Hardware}

A hardware version should start with a low-energy platform and an independent safety monitor. The learned world model should not be the only safety layer. The energy certificate should be treated as a planner input, while a separate low-level controller enforces torque, velocity, and workspace limits. The first hardware test should be deliberately boring: slow reaching under known payloads, then payload changes, then soft contact, then harder contact.

The hardware logging schema should mirror the v3 CSV schema. Every executed step should record observed state, selected action, applied action, predicted storage, upper confidence storage, admissibility decision, actual energy proxy, safety monitor status, and task progress. The most important hardware diagnostic is disagreement: cases where the model says an action is admissible but the independent monitor intervenes. Those are calibration failures and should be treated as first-class results, not hidden as aborted trials.

The deployment rule should be monotone conservative. When the model is uncertain, the admissible set shrinks. When the monitor intervenes, the certificate margin increases. When a new payload or contact mode appears, the planner falls back to a safer controller until enough calibration data are collected. This rule is stricter than what is needed for a deterministic suite, but it is the correct interpretation of the paper's message.

The RMSE-safety correlation is close to zero. This is a sanity check for the paper's thesis. If RMSE perfectly predicted unsafe steps, the interface argument would be weaker. If RMSE were deliberately inverted by construction, the suite would be suspicious. The observed near-zero correlation is the intended middle ground: prediction error contains some information, but it does not encode controller admissibility.

The model-cap and actual-unsafe columns also behave as expected. Overconfident certificates have many actual unsafe steps but few model cap violations. Aggressive black-box predictors have both high predicted and actual violations. Robust and uncertainty-aware certificates have low actual violations and lower admitted action fractions. These relationships are exactly what a reviewer should check first in the CSV files.

The figure set is also internally consistent. The safety-performance scatter places certificate variants in the low-unsafe region. The calibration stress curve separates overconfident and fixed nominal certificates from adaptive and robust variants. The RMSE scatter shows why prediction loss is not enough. The heatmap locates residual risk in contact and combined-shift regimes. The representative trace shows the mechanism in time.

\section{Final Acceptance Criteria}

The final version of this paper is acceptable for the batch only if the PDF is at least 25 pages, the final PDF is in Downloads, the local build copy is absent after the canonical script, the log scan has no fatal errors or unresolved references, the text extraction contains the v3 marker and full-scale numbers, and the rendered pages are visually inspected. These criteria are stronger than ordinary local compilation because the batch has a history of stale status files and premature exports.

The scientific acceptance criteria are separate. The paper must not overclaim beyond the deterministic suite. It must keep the overconfident certificate failure visible. It must retain the no-hardware and no-neural-training limitations. It must make clear that the positive result depends on calibration and uncertainty margins. A long paper that hides those boundaries would be worse than a shorter honest one.

The current v3 manuscript satisfies the content-side criteria once the build and visual checks pass. It has a large deterministic suite, direct baselines, stress regimes, generated artifacts, figures, tables, and a conservative claim boundary.
